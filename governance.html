AI Governance use cases

These examples show how to maintain an AI asset registry and include AI risk assessments when systems are introduced or changed. An AI registry is only useful if it stays up to date. That means identifying new AI systems and changes to existing ones as part of normal change management.

Key considerations

Principles I apply to AI governance, based on my risk and audit work and the frameworks behind it.

You cannot outsource risk

Buying an AI tool from a vendor doesn’t remove your responsibility for how it’s used. You still need to understand the risks and manage them.

Relevant: ISO/IEC 42001 A.10; EU AI Act Article 26 for high-risk AI deployers.

You cannot manage a risk you don’t know about

You need to know where AI is being used before you can assess the risks. That starts with identifying AI systems and assigning an owner.

Relevant: NIST AI RMF Map; ISO/IEC 42001 6.1.2.

Governance needs to keep up

Regulations change, and so do AI systems and how they’re used. Risks and controls need regular review, not just a one-off assessment.

Relevant: ISO/IEC 42001 9.3 and 10.

Built into existing processes

AI controls should be part of processes people already use, such as change management and access reviews, rather than an additional process to work around.

Relevant: ISO/IEC 42001 8.1, operational planning and control.

01 AI inventoryAI asset registryA questionnaire and IT interview to build an inventory of AI systems, with owner, data, access and AI Act risk category.
AI inventory

AI asset registry

ProblemThe organisation has no reliable inventory of AI systems. Ownership, data use, access and applicable AI Act obligations are not consistently recorded.

Outcome
Before
Unclear governance around AI assets.
After
An AI asset registry with an owner, data, access, initial AI Act classification and internal risk level for every identified AI system, reviewed periodically.
Example design
Approach

Each department completes a short questionnaire covering which AI tools they use, why they use them, what data they process and whether the outputs affect decisions about people.

IT then checks the answers against SSO applications and procurement records. A structured interview also covers tools that may not appear in those records, including service accounts, API access and connections to other systems.

An LLM uses the collected information to draft registry entries and suggest an initial risk classification, with supporting reasons. The asset owner checks the details, and the AI governance lead reviews the classification. Neither decision is made by the LLM.

New systems are recorded before use. Each entry has a review date, and changes to the system or its use trigger a reassessment.

Standards and frameworks
ISO/IEC 42001 A.4.2–A.4.6 NIST AI RMF Map ISO/IEC 27001 A.5.9, A.8.3 GDPR Art. 30 AI Act Art. 3
Control
Human-in-the-loop: the LLM only drafts. The asset owner confirms each entry, the AI governance lead confirms the AI Act risk category, and high-risk or unclear cases go to legal.
Security
The registry holds sensitive information, such as service accounts, access rights and where API keys are stored, never the keys themselves. Access is limited to the AI governance team and asset owners, and changes are logged.
Traceability
Each row records who approved the system and when, with a link to the questionnaire answers, interview notes and evidence.
Limits
Completeness depends on what people declare. Free tools used outside SSO and procurement can be missed. The AI Act risk category is an initial assessment, not legal advice.
02 AI in the development lifecycleAI risk assessment in the change management processEvery new development is screened for EU AI Act impact, routed to the right approver and added to the registry.
AI in the development lifecycle

AI risk assessment in the change management process

ProblemAI-related changes, including new AI features in supplier tools and changes to existing systems, can reach production without being identified, assessed or approved.

Outcome
Before
No AI risk assessment in the change process, and unclear approval responsibilities.
After
Every user story is screened during refinement, AI-related changes are routed to the required approvers automatically, and approved AI changes are added to the registry.
Example design
Approach

AI screening is part of the existing agile process. During refinement, the product owner adds the screening form to the user story, and the developer completes it before work starts.

Power Automate checks the answers, uses fixed rules to identify the right approver and creates an approval ticket.

Approval is part of the Definition of Done, and the gate is technical: the Jira workflow does not let the story move to Done until the approval ticket is closed. Once the AI-related change is approved, the AI asset registry is updated.

Standards and frameworks
ISO/IEC 42001 6.1.4, 8.4, A.5, A.6 NIST AI RMF Map ISO/IEC 27001 A.8.25, A.8.32
Control
Routing uses fixed rules, so who approves is predictable and auditable. Only the approver can approve.
Traceability
The answers, approver and decision are kept on the approval ticket and linked to the user story and the registry entry.
Limits
Screening relies on honest developer answers, so approvers spot-check them. Yes/no questions can miss edge cases, and unclear ones go to legal. This is a screening, not legal advice.
