Use cases: AI governance

AI governance from law to evidence

The following use cases show an example approach for an AI asset registry, and for building AI risk assessment into the change management lifecycle. An AI registry only stays current if new AI development is identified.

Key considerations

Principles I apply to AI governance, based on my risk and audit work and the frameworks behind it.

You cannot outsource risk

Buying an AI tool from a vendor does not hand over the risk. If something goes wrong, the company is still accountable.

Aligned with EU AI Act Art. 26, deployer obligations; ISO/IEC 42001 A.10

You cannot accept a risk you are not aware of

You can only accept a risk you know about. That is why the first step is to find every AI system and give it an owner.

Aligned with NIST AI RMF: Map; ISO/IEC 42001 6.1.2

Regulations keep changing

The AI Act was amended within two years of coming into force. Governance is reviewed continuously.

Aligned with ISO/IEC 42001 9.3 management review and 10 improvement

Built into existing processes

AI controls are built into processes people already use, such as change management and access reviews, instead of a separate process next to them.

Aligned with ISO/IEC 42001 5.1, integration into business processes

These are alignments with the concepts in each framework, not compliance claims.

01 AI inventoryAI asset registryA questionnaire and IT interview that turn scattered AI use into one registry with owners, data, access and risk class.
AI inventory

AI asset registry

ProblemThere is no complete inventory of AI assets. AI systems have no assigned owner, and it is unclear which ones process personal data, who has access to them, and which fall under the EU AI Act.

Outcome
Before
Unclear governance around AI assets.
After
An AI asset registry with an owner, data, access and risk class for every AI system.
Anonymised example
Approach

Each department fills in a short questionnaire: which AI tools they use, for what, with which data, and whether the output affects decisions about people. A structured interview with IT then checks the answers against SSO apps and procurement data, and covers what departments cannot see: who can configure each tool, which service accounts and API keys it runs on, and what other systems it can reach.

An LLM turns the answers into draft registry rows with a first AI Act class and its rationale. The business owner confirms each row and governance confirms the class. New tools enter the registry before go-live, and each row gets a review date: every 6 months for high-risk or internet-facing systems, every 12 months for the rest.

Standards and frameworks

Control
Human-in-the-loop: the LLM drafts only. The business owner confirms each row, governance confirms the AI Act class, and high-risk or unclear cases go to legal.
Traceability
Each row records who approved the system and when, with a link to the questionnaire answers, interview notes and evidence.
Limits
The registry depends on people declaring what they use. Free tools outside SSO and expenses can be missed. The AI Act class is a first assessment, not legal advice.
02 AI in the development lifecycleAI and privacy screening in change managementEvery new development is screened for privacy and EU AI Act impact, routed to the right approver and added to the registry.
AI in the development lifecycle

AI and privacy screening in change management

ProblemA registry only stays current if new AI is caught when it is built. Otherwise features with AI or personal data reach production before anyone checks them, and the review happens after go-live, if at all.

Outcome
Before
Privacy and AI review depend on someone remembering to ask, and nobody knows who should approve what.
After
Every change is screened at refinement, routed automatically to the right decision maker, and blocked at release until approved. Approved AI changes land in the registry.
Anonymised example
Approach

The screening starts at refinement, before anything is built, because privacy by design and AI Act duties are much cheaper to meet at design time than after go-live. Product owners and Scrum masters attach the form to every epic or change, and the developer answers it.

The questions follow the order a reviewer would think: first whether the AI Act applies at all, then our role, then the hard stops, then risk level, then privacy. Each answer is tied to the article that makes it matter, so the approver sees why the question was asked. Fixed rules route the change to one decision maker, who approves with conditions. The release gate checks for the approval ID, and approval creates or updates the row in the AI asset registry.

Control
Routing uses fixed rules, not AI, so who approves is predictable and auditable. The decision maker approves; the LLM only summarises and drafts.
Traceability
The answers, the articles they trigger, the approver, the conditions and the approval date stay on the change ticket and link to the registry row.
Limits
Screening relies on honest developer answers, so approvers spot-check them. Yes/no questions can miss edge cases, and unclear ones go to legal. This is a screening, not legal advice.
